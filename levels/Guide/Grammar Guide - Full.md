# Grammar Guide

## Introduction

We’re building a grammar learning game where players work as proofreaders at a law firm. Players start with Day 0, the tutorial, where they learn how to play. Then they work through seven levels, and each level is a workday that teaches a new grammar topic.

Players use what they learn each day to defeat the mini-boss: a memo that is due at 5 p.m. Each level builds on what players learned in the earlier levels while teaching them something new.

The levels are:

- Day 1: The Supply Order (Words)
- Day 2: The Exhibit List (Phrases)
- Day 3: The Client Update (Verbs)
- Day 4: Between You and Me (Roles)
- Day 5: The Witness (Clauses)
- Day 6: Where It’s At (Word Order)
- Day 7: A Matter of Commas (Punctuation)

In the final boss battle, players will proofread a court affidavit using what they learned in all seven levels.

In each level, we will look at examples we found difficult while learning in class or came across on our own. For example, Day 5 looks at when to use *who* and when to use *whom*.

## Level 1: Fewer vs. Less

***Day 1: The Supply Order (Words)***  
*Topic: fewer vs. less*

### The question
How do you know whether the office needs fewer binders or less paper? Both words describe a smaller quantity, so they’re easy to mix up.

### The rule
- Use *fewer* with things you can count one at a time.
- Use *less* with an amount you don’t count one by one (Cambridge University Press & Assessment, n.d.; Oxford University Press, n.d.).

### Example
- Correct Use: Please order fewer binders and less paper.
- **Incorrect Use: Please order less binders. → *fewer* binders**

### Explanation
Ask yourself, “How many?” or “How much?”

| Ask | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| How many? | *fewer* | fewer binders |
| How much? | *less* | less glue |

- *Binders* is a plural countable noun because you can count each binder.
- *Paper* is a non-countable noun here because it means the material, not separate documents.
- *Fewer* and *less* are determiners: they show a smaller number or amount.

### Tricky cases
- Use *less* with measurements of time, money, and distance:
  - *less than three hours*
  - *less than $50*
  - *less than ten kilometres*
- Use *fewer* for individual people and things: *fewer than ten clients*.
- If *paper* means separate documents, it can be counted: *fewer papers*.

### Unique approach
**The Supply Order Under Cross-Examination**

Ms. Fewster: “Can we count the binders one by one?”  
Clerk: “Yes.”  
Ms. Fewster: “Then use *fewer binders*. And the paper?”  
Clerk: “We measure it as an amount.”  
Ms. Fewster: “Then use *less paper*.”

### Skeptic’s corner
You may hear people say *less binders* in everyday conversation, and most people will understand them. For formal proofreading, use *fewer* with countable items and save *less* for amounts and measurements.

Grammarphobia traces this rule to 1770, when grammarian Robert Baker argued that *fewer* was more proper than *less* in a phrase like “no less than a hundred.” The article notes that writers had used *less* to mean *fewer* for centuries before then (O’Conner & Kellerman, 2010).

### Practice
1. The office needs ___ folders than it ordered last month.
   - (A) fewer
   - (B) less
   - **Answer: A.** You can count folders one by one.

2. The courier spent ___ than $50 delivering the boxes.
   - (A) fewer
   - (B) less
   - **Answer: B.** Fifty dollars is an amount of money.

3. The office used ___ paper than last month but filed ___ papers.
   - (A) less / fewer
   - (B) fewer / less
   - **Answer: A.** *Paper* means the material, so it’s non-countable. *Papers* means separate documents, so you can count them.

4. The clerk finished the review in ___ than 90 minutes and found ___ errors than the first reviewer.
   - (A) less / fewer
   - (B) fewer / less
   - **Answer: A.** Use *less* for a measurement of time and *fewer* for countable errors.

### Inspect passage
*Email from Omar Haddad, Senior Paralegal. Subject: Courier budget update. There are two errors.*

> We had less courier deliveries this month than last month, so the invoice was lower. The courier charged fewer than $200 for the deliveries. We also used less paper by sending receipts electronically. Please review the attached summary before I send it to Ms. Fewster.

**Answer key**
1. *less courier deliveries* → *fewer*: deliveries can be counted one by one.
2. *fewer than $200* → *less*: money is an amount.
- Trap: *less paper* is correct, because paper is the material here, an amount.

### Mini-boss

*Memo from Ms. Fewster, Managing Partner. Re: Supplies for tomorrow’s hearing. It must be corrected before the supplier’s 5 p.m. cutoff. There are three errors.*

> Please order less binders than we ordered for the last hearing. The copy room has fewer toner than usual, so add one cartridge to the order. We need less than 12 copies of the revised affidavit. The supplier says delivery will take less than two hours, so the supplies should arrive before the hearing.

**Answer key**
1. *less binders* → *fewer*: binders are individual items you can count.
2. *fewer toner* → *less*: toner is an amount of material here.
3. *less than 12 copies* → *fewer*: copies are individual items you can count.
- Trap: *less than two hours* is correct, because hours measure a length of time.

## Level 2: Subject–Verb Agreement

***Day 2: The Exhibit List (Phrases)***  
*Topic: subject–verb agreement*

### The question
Day 1 focused on individual words. Day 2 focuses on phrases because a phrase can add information to the subject and make it harder to find the noun that controls the verb. In *the schedule for interviews*, *for interviews* adds detail, but the main noun, called the *head*, is *schedule*. That’s why we write “The schedule for interviews is posted at reception.”

Which verb fits? “The notes from the court reporter **is/are** ready for filing.” *Reporter* is closest to the verb, but *notes* is the head.

### The rule
Find the head of the subject, then match the verb to it (Cambridge University Press & Assessment, n.d.). A phrase such as *from the court reporter* adds information, but it doesn’t change whether *notes* is singular or plural (The Writing Lab & OWL at Purdue University, n.d.).

### Example
- Correct Use: The contract for the new office **is** ready.
- **Incorrect Use: The contract for the new office are ready. → *is***

### Explanation
A *phrase* is a group of words built around one main word, called its *head*. Other words or phrases that add information to the head are its *dependents*. In *the contract for the new office*, *contract* is the head, and *for the new office* is a prepositional phrase that describes it. This phrase is an *adjectival dependent* (Watson, 2023a).

Find the head of the subject. A phrase beginning with *of*, *for*, or *with* can add detail, but a noun inside that phrase usually doesn’t control the verb. Try crossing out the phrase and reading the head with the verb.

| Ask | Head | Verb |
|---|---|---|
| What’s the head? | *contract* (one) | *is* |
| What’s the head? | *signatures* (more than one) | *are* |

- The deadline for the filings **is** approaching. → The deadline **is** approaching.
- The signatures on the release form **are** missing. → The signatures **are** missing.

### Tricky cases
*And* joins two subjects, so it takes a plural verb. *Along with* adds information but doesn’t join another subject (The Writing Lab & OWL at Purdue University, n.d.).

- The clerk **and** two couriers **are** waiting outside.
- The clerk, **along with** two couriers, **is** waiting outside.

One exception is *a number of*. When it means *several*, use a plural verb: “A number of hearing dates **have** changed.” Likewise, when *all of* or *some of* refers to plural items, the verb is plural: “All of the pages **are** signed.”

### Unique approach
**The Subject Under Cross-Examination**

Ms. Fewster: “Your memo says, ‘The envelope for the signed copies are on my desk.’ Which noun is closest to the verb?”  
Clerk: “*Copies*, so I chose *are*.”  
Ms. Fewster: “Cross out *for the signed copies*. What’s left?”  
Clerk: “The envelope is on my desk. The head is *envelope*, so the verb should be *is*.”

### Skeptic’s corner
Crossing out a phrase is only a way to find the subject. It doesn’t mean the phrase is unnecessary; it may carry important information. After choosing the verb, read the full sentence again to make sure the added phrase still fits.

Watson (2023b) describes the present-tense *-s* as a leftover from older English. Modern English shows subject agreement in only a few places: most verbs mark it only in the present (*she bites*; *they bite*), while most past-tense verbs and modal verbs such as *can* and *will* don’t change form to match the subject. So is the rule worth the fuss? Even a small verb ending can help readers connect a subject to its verb, especially when a phrase comes between them.

### Practice
1. The typo in the footnotes ___ easy to overlook.
   - (A) is
   - (B) are
   - **Answer: A.** *Typo* is the head. *Footnotes* is inside a phrase, so it doesn’t control the verb.

2. The dates in the margin ___ written in pencil.
   - (A) is
   - (B) are
   - **Answer: B.** *Dates* is plural, so it takes *are*. The singular noun *margin* is inside the phrase.

3. The handwritten note, together with two corrections, ___ in red ink.
   - (A) appears
   - (B) appear
   - **Answer: A.** The head is *note*. *Together with two corrections* adds information but doesn’t make the subject plural.

4. The reviewer and the transcriptionist ___ the totals before filing.
   - (A) checks
   - (B) check
   - **Answer: B.** *And* joins two subjects, so the plural verb *check* is correct.

### Inspect passage
*Email from Alina Rao, Records Clerk. Subject: Page labels. There are two errors.*

> The label on the scanned pages are too small. The initials on the final sheet needs to match the filing record. I can update them before the packet is sent to the court.

**Answer key**
1. *are* → *is*: *label* is the singular head; *pages* is inside a phrase.
2. *needs* → *need*: *initials* is plural; *sheet* is inside a phrase.

### Mini-boss

*Memo from Ms. Fewster, Managing Partner. Re: Affidavit packet. It must be ready for filing by 5 p.m. There are three errors and one correct trap.*

> The total of the replacement pages were added to the invoice. The approval pages, together with the cover sheet, is ready for signature. The chief clerk, as well as two vendors, are expected at the review. The client notice and the revised schedule are attached to this memo.

**Answer key**
1. *were* → *was*: *total* is the singular head.
2. *is* → *are*: *approval pages* is the plural subject; *together with the cover sheet* doesn’t change it.
3. *are* → *is*: *chief clerk* is singular; *as well as two vendors* doesn’t change it.
- Trap: *The client notice and the revised schedule are* is correct because *and* joins two subjects.

## Level 3: Good/Well, Bad/Badly

***Day 3: The Client Update (Verbs)***  
*Topic: good/well and bad/badly*

### The question
Should you write that a client update sounds *good* or *well*? The right word depends on whether the verb connects the subject to a description or tells what someone does.

### The rule
- Use the adjectives *good* and *bad* after linking verbs to describe the subject.
- Use the adverbs *well* and *badly* to describe an action (Cambridge University Press & Assessment, n.d.-a, n.d.-b).

### Example
- Correct Use: The revised wording sounds *good*.
- **Incorrect Use: The revised wording sounds *well*. → *good***
- Correct Use: The assistant explained the change *well*.
- **Incorrect Use: The assistant explained the change *good*. → *well***

### Explanation
Linking verbs are *stative* verbs: they express a state rather than an action. An adjective after a linking verb is a *predicative complement* because it describes or renames the subject. An adverb such as *well* can be a *modifier* of an action verb. In “The new heading seems bad,” *bad* is the predicative complement; in “The assistant explained the change well,” *well* modifies the action verb (Cambridge University Press & Assessment, n.d.-a, n.d.-c).

To use the *be test*, try replacing the verb with *is* or *was*. If the sentence still makes sense, use an adjective after the verb. For example, “The new heading seems bad” still makes sense as “The new heading is bad.”

### Tricky cases
- *Well* can also be an adjective meaning healthy. Both “Are you feeling well?” and “Are you feeling good?” are correct: *well* asks about health, while *good* asks about mood or general condition. The mediator felt well after recovering from a cold.
- Some verbs can be linking verbs or action verbs, depending on how they are used. In “The print looks bad,” *looks* connects the subject to a description. In “The editor looked closely at the print,” *looked* describes an action.

### Unique approach
**The Verb on the Stand**

Ms. Fewster: “Your memo says, ‘The new paragraph seems badly.’ Is *seems* describing an action?”  
Clerk: “No. I can try the be test: ‘The paragraph is bad’ works, so *seems* is a linking verb.”  
Ms. Fewster: “Right. *Seems* links the subject to a description, so write ‘The paragraph seems bad.’”  
Clerk: “And if the assistant explained the paragraph good?”  
Ms. Fewster: “*Explained* is an action, so use the adverb *well*.”

### Skeptic’s corner
You may hear “I did good” when someone means they performed well. In formal writing, use *well* to describe how an action was done. The phrase *do good* is also correct when it means to help people or make things better.

People also commonly say “I feel badly for your mom,” perhaps because the *-ly* ending sounds more correct. In “I feel bad for your mom,” *feel* is a linking verb and *bad* describes the speaker’s feeling. In formal proofreading, use *feel bad* for sympathy or regret. *Feel badly* can also be literal when it means someone has a poor sense of touch (Cambridge University Press & Assessment, n.d.-a, n.d.-c).

### Practice
1. The editor described the revised schedule ___.
   - (A) good
   - (B) well
   - **Answer: B.** *Described* is an action verb, so use the adverb *well*.

2. The new client portal looks ___ after the update.
   - (A) good
   - (B) well
   - **Answer: A.** *Looks* links the portal to a description, so use the adjective *good*.

3. The coordinator handled the last-minute change ___.
   - (A) bad
   - (B) badly
   - **Answer: B.** *Handled* is an action verb, so use the adverb *badly*.

4. The revised explanation seems ___ to the client.
   - (A) bad
   - (B) badly
   - **Answer: A.** *Seems* is a linking verb here, so use the adjective *bad*.

### Inspect passage
*Email from Noor Awan, Legal Assistant. Subject: Client update. There are two errors.*

> The revised summary seems badly after the new details were added. The coordinator explained the fee change good before sending the email.

**Answer key**
1. *badly* → *bad*: *seems* links *summary* to a description.
2. *good* → *well*: *explained* is an action verb.

### Mini-boss

*Memo from Ms. Fewster, Managing Partner. Re: Client update draft. It must be ready before the 5 p.m. client call. There are three errors and one correct trap.*

> The assistant presented the revised fee good during the call. The print on the revised notice looks badly under the new lighting. The researcher handled the request bad. The client’s first impression was good.

**Answer key**
1. *good* → *well*: *presented* is an action verb.
2. *badly* → *bad*: *looks* links *print* to a description.
3. *bad* → *badly*: *handled* is an action verb.
- Trap: *The client’s first impression was good* is correct because *good* describes the subject after the linking verb *was*.

## Level 4: Pronoun Case

***Day 4: Between You and Me (Roles)***  
*Topic: subject and object pronouns*

### The question
The first three days focused on words, phrases, and verbs. Day 4 focuses on the role a pronoun plays in a sentence. A pronoun can be the subject that does the action, the object that receives it, or the object of a preposition. The role determines which form to use.

Would you write “Rina and I will review the notice” or “The registrar sent the notice to Rina and me”? Both are correct. The pronoun changes form because its role in the sentence changes.

### The rule
- Use subject forms such as *I, she, he, we,* and *they* for the subject of a verb.
- Use object forms such as *me, her, him, us,* and *them* after a verb or preposition (Cambridge University Press & Assessment, n.d.-a; The Writing Lab & OWL at Purdue University, n.d.).
- Use reflexive forms such as *myself* when they refer back to the subject or add emphasis; don’t use them just to sound more formal (Cambridge University Press & Assessment, n.d.-b).

### Example
- Correct Use: Laila and I reviewed the transmittal letter.
- **Incorrect Use: Laila and me reviewed the transmittal letter. → *I***
- Correct Use: The docket clerk sent the confirmation to Laila and me.
- **Incorrect Use: The docket clerk sent the confirmation to Laila and I. → *me***

### Explanation
Subject pronouns are in the subjective case; they identify who or what the sentence is about. Object pronouns are in the objective case; they receive an action or follow a preposition. In a phrase with more than one person, cover the other name and check the pronoun by itself: “The attorney thanked Celia and me” becomes “The attorney thanked me,” not “The attorney thanked I.”

### Tricky cases
- Use a reflexive pronoun when the subject and object are the same person: “The assistant reminded herself to save the letter.” For emphasis, you can also write, “The assistant herself saved the letter.” Don’t use *myself* instead of *me*: “Please send the final copy to me,” not “to myself.”
- In a formal comparison, use the subject form when the pronoun is the subject of an understood verb: “The mediator spoke longer than I did.” In conversation, *than me* is common, but completing the clause makes the pronoun’s role clear (The Writing Lab & OWL at Purdue University, n.d.).

### Unique approach
**The Drop-the-Name Check**

Ms. Fewster: “Your draft says, ‘The analyst copied the client and I.’ What if we cover *the client*?”  
Clerk: “The analyst copied *I*. That sounds wrong; the object form is *me*.”  
Ms. Fewster: “So how should the sentence read?”  
Clerk: “The analyst copied the client and me.”

### Skeptic’s corner
People are often corrected for saying “me and the auditor” as a subject, so “the auditor and I” can start to feel like the formal choice everywhere. But the pronoun still has to match its role. After *between*, use the object form: “between the auditor and me” (The Writing Lab & OWL at Purdue University, n.d.).

### Practice
1. The mediator and ___ will organize the record index.
   - (A) I
   - (B) me
   - **Answer: A.** *I* is part of the subject.

2. The litigation assistant forwarded the image to the court clerk and ___.
   - (A) I
   - (B) me
   - **Answer: B.** *To* is a preposition, so use the object form *me*.

3. The files were divided between the docket manager and ___.
   - (A) I
   - (B) me
   - **Answer: B.** *Between* is a preposition, so use *me*.

4. The clerk completed the form more quickly than ___ did.
   - (A) I
   - (B) me
   - **Answer: A.** *I* is the subject of the understood comparison clause: “I did.”

5. I reminded ___ to attach the final receipt.
   - (A) me
   - (B) myself
   - **Answer: B.** The subject *I* is reminding the same person, so the reflexive form *myself* is correct.

### Inspect passage
*Email from Farah Nasser, Office Coordinator. Subject: Service packet. There are two errors.*

> The docket manager and me checked the delivery schedule this morning. The revised copy was returned to the registrar and I. The office coordinator herself saved the clean version.

**Answer key**
1. *me* → *I*: *I* is part of the subject.
2. *I* → *me*: *to* is a preposition and needs the object form.
- Trap: *The office coordinator herself saved the clean version* is correct; *herself* adds emphasis.

### Mini-boss

*Memo from Ms. Fewster, Managing Partner. Re: Service packet. It must be ready for the 5 p.m. filing. There are three errors and one correct trap.*

> Her and I will prepare the index tabs before the 5 p.m. filing. The signed order was sent to the court registrar and I. The docket assistant left a reminder for myself. The senior associate finished the checklist sooner than I expected.

**Answer key**
1. *Her* → *She*: *she* is part of the subject.
2. *I* → *me*: *to* is a preposition and needs the object form.
3. *myself* → *me*: *myself* needs to refer back to *I* or add emphasis; here, use the object form *me*.
- Trap: *The senior associate finished the checklist sooner than I expected* is correct; *I* is the subject of *expected*.
